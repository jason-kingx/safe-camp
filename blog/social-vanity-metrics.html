Meta's Own Research: Engagement Doesn't Correlate with Sales

By Jason King  ·  May 2026  ·  15 min read

TL;DR

For well over a decade, research from Nielsen, Facebook, Datalogix, comScore, and others has consistently reached the same conclusion: social media engagement metrics — likes, comments, shares, and click-through rates — do not correlate with brand growth, offline sales, or return on advertising spend (ROAS).

Facebook's own VP of Marketing Science called engagement data "irrelevant" and said it has "no more chance of predicting actual business outcomes than a random guess."

Yet today, many marketers and social media professionals still optimize for these vanity metrics as if they were proof of business success. This article consolidates the research that proves otherwise.

Key Points
  • Organic Engagement: Typical organic vanity metrics (organic impressions, likes, comments, shares, clicks) do not correlate with sales volume or return on ad spend (ROAS).
  • Clicks: Click-through rate is not the right metric to measure brand impact. There is virtually no relationship between clicks (and click-through rates) and brand opinion metrics or offline sales.
  • Advertising: Online ads can succeed in driving brand impact, though success is not guaranteed. Advertisers must embrace new online branding metrics to separate themselves from the competition. Reviewing paid impressions and other paid sequence metrics can be actionable, predictable for forward-looking simulations, and help in cross-channel comparison.
  • Brand Impact: There is emerging evidence that brand metrics — which show attitudinal response to online campaigns — are correlated with and can predict offline sales impact.

The Groundwork for Effective Marketing

In 2006, the US Advertising Research Foundation published an official definition of consumer engagement as the process of a prospect being "turned on" to a brand idea — one in which consumers don't just receive a message, but add their own associations and meanings to it.

That framing positioned "engagement" as something fundamentally richer than traditional message delivery. It was philosophically compelling — after all, who wouldn't want consumers actively co-creating meaning with their brand?

In 2007, Forrester Research declared "engagement" as "marketing's new key metric." Forrester's definition was considerably more thoughtful than what the term came to represent. Their framework organized engagement across four dimensions:

  1. Involvement (attention and time spent)
  2. Interaction (actions taken)
  3. Intimacy (the emotional closeness a consumer feels toward a brand over time)
  4. Influence (the likelihood of recommending it)

These dimensions are represented by brand studies, through measures like awareness, attention, affinity, and recall.

They are not represented by social media platforms' typical vanity metrics: likes, comments, shares, or clicks.

Unfortunately, such foundations of effective marketing measurement quickly became skewed, and have remained skewed ever since.

The Metrics That Continue Misleading Marketers

When it became first possible to advertise on social media, there were no perfect measurement solutions. Reach was hard to measure precisely. Sales attribution barely existed within and across channels. In the same year as Forrester's research, Facebook included engagement data in its first Ads dashboard.

Due to Facebook's early social media dominance, other social platforms adopted similar language for "engagement." Marketers needed something to report, so they included the numbers they could grab: likes, shares, comments, and clicks were right there — easy to pull and present in a slide deck.

They were intuitively satisfying, since "engagements" and "likes" sound like the mental shortcut, "our audiences like us!" They felt like proof that people cared.

Unfortunately, they did not truly represent brand or conversion impact from their inception. They were also easy to inflate, miscalculate, or misrepresent. Although Forrester Research and social media platforms used similar language, the social platforms won.

What Facebook Said About Its Own Metrics

Perhaps the most striking aspect from more than a decade of research and social media activity comes from the platform that set these issues into motion: Facebook itself. The company most responsible for making "engagement" the center of social media marketing has actually been publishing research against it for years.

The problem is that these findings were buried in white papers and webinars. Most marketers did not see the research, and of those that did, many forgot about it. At the same time, engagement counters remained front and center in organic and paid dashboards across social media platforms.

In a 2014 white paper titled Engagement on Facebook: When It Matters, Facebook laid out the problem plainly: online engagement metrics are a proxy for interest, but they are not a reliable indicator of content's persuasiveness. Persuasive content influences your audience in a way that helps move your business — but likes, comments, shares, and clicks are not persuasion.

The paper introduced a useful framework. When someone engages with a social media post, it means they 1) noticed it, 2) found it interesting, and 4) chose to express that. You are probably thinking, why is the third step omitted? That step is actual persuasion — the attitude or intention shift that drives business outcomes — and it is optional according to the research. Content doesn't need to be persuasive to elicit an "engagement." And persuasive content frequently generates no engagement at all, especially for brand awareness campaigns. The two things are simply not reliably connected.

Facebook went even further, pointing out that engagement rates often reflect the user's tendency rather than the content's quality or relevance. There are "lurkers," "engagers," and "watchers" — people with fundamentally different clicking habits — and the same people who have low engagement rates with their friends' posts are also unlikely to engage with advertising content. Low engagement doesn't mean people weren't influenced. High engagement doesn't mean they were.

Facebook's internal analysis, based on US users in January 2014, found a meaningful correlation between engagement rates on friend content and engagement rates on advertising content. This confirmed that ad engagement reflects the person, not the persuasiveness of the ad.

The paper's key takeaways were clear and unambiguous: engagement should not be a campaign goal, nor an action to optimize for. If you are trying to generate demand or change perceptions about your product, engagement metrics should not be used to assess your advertising effectiveness. They are helpful in a small way, the paper acknowledges, but not sufficient — because people can be persuaded by content even if they never engage with it.

The same paper warned against "like baiting" (today called engagement baiting) — page posts built entirely to generate likes or shares, with no actual brand messaging. Facebook noted that such posts derail analysis because they lack brand content and are unlikely to persuade anyone of anything. They can also reflect poorly on the brand. You've likely seen many examples of such content, falling into "blanding," memes, trends, holidays, and filler posts.

Brad Smallwood, Facebook's Vice President of Marketing Science, was even more direct in other contexts. Published research attributed to him states there is "no link" between trendy online engagement measures and ad effectiveness. He called engagement data "irrelevant" and said it has no more predictive power over actual business outcomes than a random guess. Facebook's case studies, he noted, almost always feature business metrics like sales and awareness — and almost never highlight engagement — for precisely this reason.

As of 2021, Facebook's own internal measurement approach had shifted decisively toward marketing mix modeling (MMM), and away from click-based attribution entirely. Internal data shared in Facebook's own webinars showed a correlation of R² = 0.0001 between engagement metrics and sales. For non-statisticians: that is essentially zero. Engagement and sales are statistically unrelated — not statistically significant.

What Nielsen's Research Found

While Facebook was publishing cautions from the inside, Nielsen was reaching the same conclusions from the outside. In October 2011, Nielsen published Beyond Clicks and Impressions: Examining the Relationship Between Online Advertising and Brand Building. The report drew on Nielsen's U.S. online advertising effectiveness data and reached three main conclusions.

  1. Clicks: Click-through rate is not the right metric to measure brand impact. There is virtually no relationship between clicks and brand opinion metrics or offline sales.
  2. Brand Metrics: There is emerging evidence that brand metrics — measures of attitudinal response to an online campaign, such as ad recall, message awareness, and purchase consideration — are correlated with, and can predict, offline sales impact.
  3. Advertising: Online ads can succeed in driving brand impact, but success is not guaranteed. Advertisers must adopt new online branding metrics to separate themselves from the competition.

The study connected brand engagement results — specifically increases in purchase intent after viewing an online ad — with actual offline sales measured by Nielsen. The correlation was real. When purchase intent rose, offline sales rose. But click-through rate showed no such connection to sales lift, and no measure of whether the message resonated with consumers.

Nielsen also published research specifically on clicks versus offline sales. The findings showed very limited correlation between clicks and offline sales lift, and equally limited correlation between clicks and standard brand metrics like awareness. More than 90% of offline sales, according to a 2012 Facebook and Datalogix ROI study, come from people who don't interact with ads during a campaign.

Think about what that means: the people who click are a small, self-selected group of naturally click-prone users — and they are not who is buying your product.

Engagement Doesn't Represent Your Audience

By now, you understand from research by Facebook and Nielsen that likes, comments, shares, and clicks don't correlate to brand or conversion impact. And even in the case of social media advertising, optimizing for engagement doesn't just fail to improve campaigns — it may actually make them perform worse.

But even more research supported these findings.

BBDO's analysis of major brand content on Facebook and Instagram increasingly showed that the people engaging with brand content were registering outside of the target audience the brand actually wanted to reach.

A layer of this problem: Audience Segments

For example, a premium building materials brand had a social media presence with several thousand followers, large engagement numbers, and an Instagram account that produced visually beautiful content. The engagement metrics looked strong on the surface. But when the audience was examined, the majority of followers were in their early twenties — nowhere near the age or income profile of a person who renovates a home with premium cladding. Many followers weren't even in the country where the product was sold. The engagement metrics looked great. The business impact was near zero.

A layer of this problem: Age

Research found that engagement rate increases dramatically with age. In one Facebook and Instagram brand study, the engagement rate for users under 34 was 3%, while the highest engagers were users aged 64 and older, at 22%. For a brand targeting younger consumers, optimizing for engagement would have been optimizing for the wrong audience entirely. Estimated ad recall lift, however, performed consistently at or above benchmarks across all age groups.

A layer of this problem: Format

Telecom provider Cox Communications found that image ads generated less social engagement than other creative formats, so it removed them from its ad buys. Engagement went up. Then Cox started tracking conversions from social — and discovered that image ads had driven more revenue than the other formats it had kept. By optimizing for engagement, Cox had actively removed its best-performing ads.

This is the heart of what makes "engagement" so dangerous as a primary metric: it's not just one issue, but many. Engagement doesn't show you who your ad actually influenced. It only shows you who clicked — and those may be very different people.

The analogies I like to use are that:

  • Engagements are like the interactive parts of a Bop It toy. Whether you bop it, like it, laugh it, or click it — the count is a distraction.
  • Engagements are like the TV series Whose Line Is It Anyway? The points don't matter.

"Dunk in the Dark" and the Myth of Virality

When the power went out during Super Bowl XLVII in 2013, the Oreo marketing team tweeted "you can still dunk in the dark" from its social media command center; this was retweeted almost 15,000 times and increased Oreo's count of Twitter, Facebook, and Instagram followers. One commentator remarked that this "solidified the viability and necessity of real-time marketing." The tweet became a landmark of social media marketing case studies and launched hundreds of "social media war rooms" devoted to real-time content creation.

Jerry Daykin, former Head of Social Media at Mondelez (the company that owns Oreo), was an insider to the actual numbers. He has written that Dunk in the Dark was part of a wider print campaign, and that Oreo also ran a conventional Super Bowl ad. The earned reach of the tweet — the people who actually saw it — was somewhere between 500,000 and 1 million.

Oreo sells hundreds of millions of cookies in the US each year. The viral moment that supposedly defined modern social marketing reached a fraction of a fraction of the brand's potential customer base. Subsequent Oreo campaigns with substantial paid media behind them were considerably more effective.

Dunk in the Dark was a clever piece of real-time marketing, but it was not proof that engagement-first strategies drive brand growth. It launched a thousand wrong strategies and convinced a generation of marketers that virality was a viable path to business success.

The Consequences of Engagement for CMOs

The research didn't just question and disprove social media engagement metrics. It explained why their prevalence has been actively harmful to the credibility of social media marketing as a discipline.

Reporting engagement data instead of deeper brand, conversions, and business results has made it harder for comms leaders to trust social media. Less than one in four CMOs, according to figures cited in the research, say they can prove the value of social media.

Sprout Social data cited in the research found that 55% of marketers still can't measure social media ROI — making it their top social challenge. The engagement-first culture has contributed directly to that gap by teaching the wrong optimization targets, building dashboards around the wrong signals, and establishing the wrong definition of success throughout the industry.

Marketing leaders in other disciplines — search, email, display — routinely report CMO-friendly metrics: leads, sales, lifetime value, attributable revenue. Social media managers have historically reported engagement rates, while the same executives sit across the table wondering what that actually means for the business.

On the other side, reporting engagement data has decreased bandwidth of social media professionals, while misrepresenting the effectiveness of that content.

A senior sporting goods marketer quoted in the research put it bluntly:

"In 2016 we had our most engagement ever, but sales were soft. We have seen significant value since dropping engagement as a core metric."

The Bigger Picture: Media Attention Was Fragmenting

Multi-screen research from Facebook IQ (2018) adds important context to why the vanity metrics problem is compounded in the media environment.

As TV viewership has fragmented, consumer attention has fragmented with it. People watch 40+ hours of TV per week, but they're also on their phones. An eye-tracking study commissioned by Facebook and conducted by Tobii Pro found that only about half of 15-second TV ads were watched for more than two seconds. Second-screen usage goes up 50% during TV commercial breaks, according to research from the Center for Research Excellence and Nielsen.

In that fractured attention environment, counting clicks as evidence of brand building becomes even less meaningful. The 90%+ of offline sales that come from non-clickers are people whose attention your ad captured — even briefly, even while they were scrolling — and who were influenced without registering any trackable engagement. Those people are invisible in a social analytics dashboard that only shows you who clicked.

Why This Still Matters Today

It would be easy to assume this is all just old news. The research findings are 10 to 15 years old in some cases. Surely the industry has moved on?

The evidence suggests otherwise.

Social media platforms still prominently surface engagement metrics as the default reporting view. A variety of in-platform notifications and alerts suggest that content is "performing" on the basis of engagement. Social media management tools and their companies' studies also often lead with engagement rate as a performance benchmark. Agency decks still celebrate viral posts and high-engagement content as examples of successful campaigns. Thought leaders still build entire personal brands and businesses on the promise that organic engagement is the path to marketing growth.

There remains a structural incentive problem. Engagement is easy to generate, easy to report, and emotionally satisfying in a way that brand lift percentages and marketing mix model coefficients are not. A post that gets 10,000 likes feels like a win. It's like a personal dopamine spike. A campaign that moved purchase intent by 4 points, or added $1.2 million in attributable offline sales — that takes more work to explain, and more investment to measure. This is exactly why engagement metrics are called "vanity metrics."

In 2012, Alan Mitchell described the deeper reason why this persists as "brand narcissism" — the occupational hazard of assuming that consumers' lives do — or should — revolve around the brand.

The marketer's actual job is to understand how the brand improves the metrics of consumers' lives. Engagement culture inverts that. It turns the exercise into identifying how consumers can improve the brand's metrics — through likes, shares, comments, and clicks that serve the brand's dashboards far more than they serve any consumer need.

As Ben Hammersley, then editor-at-large of Wired UK, put it the same year: the idea that people want to spend their time engaging with branded content is a myth. Most consumers think about a brand once — when they're buying it.

The research has been consistent for over a decade.

Social engagements and clicks do not build brands. They do not correlate with sales. They are not a proxy for persuasion.

Organic impressions and organic engagement also rank poorly as planning metrics. The research notes that paid impressions are more actionable, more predictable for forward-looking simulations, and more useful for cross-channel comparison. Organic reach on major platforms is increasingly throttled, inconsistent, and audience-mismatched in ways that make it unreliable as a baseline for planning. In a separate article, I dissect the many pitfalls of organic reach.

The sooner social media marketing — as a profession — internalizes these findings and shifts its measurement standards accordingly, the sooner it will earn the credibility it has spent years struggling to claim.

What Actually Predicts Business Outcomes

If not engagement, what should marketers be measuring? The research identifies clear frameworks.

For business and social media planning

Content production and other social media activities should be decided, produced, and measured based on a different logic. Specifically, business, marketing, and social media objectives, as well as insights gained from customer research and brand studies.

  • Measure social media campaigns against business objectives, using the same KPIs that govern every other marketing channel. If TV and email are measured on sales lift and brand recall, social should be measured the same way. Success should be demonstrated through business results — not through engagement rates.
  • Align creative to business objectives, not to engagement. Creative assets built solely to generate engagement — "like baiting" — will not drive core business results, and may actively undermine them by lacking brand messaging. Build ads to grab attention, convey a brand message, and connect to a business goal. Measure whether people remembered the ad and whether it shifted their perception of the brand.

For brand campaigns

The meaningful metrics are brand recall, message association, ad recall lift, awareness lift, purchase intent, and favorability. These attitudinal measures — often captured through tools like Social Media Brand Studies or Nielsen Brand Effect studies — have been shown to correlate with actual offline sales. They measure whether someone's opinion of a brand or product changed after seeing an ad. That change is what drives eventual purchase behavior. A click does not.

For conversion and sales campaigns

The appropriate measures are actual conversions, conversion or sales lift studies (including offline), return on advertising spend, offline sales attribution, and marketing mix modeling — a methodology Facebook itself adopted as its primary internal measurement approach. Depending on ad spend, it may be possible to run individual or combined brand and conversion lift studies.

For reach and frequency

Facebook IQ's 2018 cross-screen study points to the importance of people-based measurement rather than cookie-based counting.

  • A Datalicious study commissioned by Facebook in Australia found that the average person had three unique cookie identifiers, meaning cookie-based measurement was simultaneously overestimating reach and underestimating frequency. Neither is useful for making sound media decisions.
  • Remember the distinction between "watchers" and "engagers." Every brand's audience contains both. The non-clickers are not failing to respond to your advertising. They may be exactly the people who are most influenced by it.

For your customers

This article will end with the way it started: marketing foundations. Remember that most consumers don't want a relationship with your brand.

Harvard Business School professor Clayton Christensen's "jobs to be done" framework is useful here: consumers hire brands to accomplish specific tasks — choosing a deodorant, opening a bank account, buying cladding for a renovation — and most of those tasks don't require ongoing engagement before or after the purchase.

Designing campaigns around the assumption that consumers want to co-create, participate, or build a relationship with a brand substitutes the brand's agenda for the consumer's.

The question isn't "how do we get more engagement?" It's "did the right people see this, and did it shift how they think about the product? Did it move them to purchase?"
